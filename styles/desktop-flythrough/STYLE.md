# Desktop Flythrough

> App screens and tools lie on a sunlit desk as printed paper cards; small pixel characters, popped up into solid toys, carry the work from card to card, while one unbroken camera flies after them, whips between stops and finally pulls back over the whole desk.
> References (grammar only): one-take desktop tours in software launch films (windows laid flat as places, a small helper as the guide); macro toy photography (low warm sun, long soft shadows); pixel-art sprites (the cast's look). Never copy their characters, layouts or icons.

## 1. Essence, and what it is not

- **The desk is the world.** A matte paper mat with a regular dot grid lies on a light wooden desk, lit by one low warm sun through a window. Everything sits on it and casts a long, soft shadow. A few everyday desk things (a pen, sticky notes, a mug, a small plant) sit around the edges as foreground and background layers.
- **Interfaces are paper cards.** Phones, tools and dashboards are rounded slabs a few millimetres thick with the interface printed on top. They are places: characters stand and hop on them, and parts of the interface rise out of them as small solid objects.
- **The cast are pixel sprites made solid.** One small block per pixel, thicker inside than at the edge, eyes standing proud. They lie on a card as flat stickers and pop up into toys; they blink, wait with a small bounce, get squashed and spring back.
- **Every stop is busy.** At each station something small moves besides the main action: debris bursts, data cubes hop, a lamp blinks, marks vibrate.
- **One take.** No cuts. The camera glides, arcs, cranes and whips between stops; it closes on the whole desk.
- **Everything lands on the pulse.** Hops, pops, ticks and slams sit on the quarters of one steady pulse, and every action has a sound.

Not a screen recording (no cursor, no glowing monitor, no system chrome); not a brick or clay toy film (pixel blocks on printed paper); not an isometric infographic (a perspective camera, real light); not flat motion graphics (everything has thickness and a shadow).

## 2. Materials & rendering

- **Scale**: a card is a hand-sized thing on a desk; dots sit a fingertip apart. A character must be big enough to show its eyes: about a fifth to a third of the card it stands on; smaller casts lose their faces in a medium shot. A character may be scaled up for a moment when the story needs it; it reads best on a pulse, with a pop's overshoot.
- **Light**: one sun low from the back corner (about 20° up, so a toy's shadow is two or three times its height); shadows read cool blue and lit faces warm; a light haze thins the far desk.
- **Desk**: a dotted paper mat (a thin rounded slab) on light oak (planks, long soft grain). Soft window-light patches (blurred four-pane shapes, warm, low opacity) lie under the cards. Props are plain shapes in matte or satin finishes, placed so each close shot has something soft in front of or behind its subject.
- **Cards and screens**: a rounded slab with flat print on top and a faint paper grain: one ink, mono labels in spaced capitals, bold numerals, thin rules; a card head carries a small label over a bold name. A phone is a card with a dark bezel and a whole interface: a status bar, a header that names the screen, content filling the screen, controls at the bottom.
- **The cast**: depth grows with distance from the silhouette; pure-black pixels are eyes, which stand proud and blink. Rig: feet and turn → hinge (lying flat ↔ standing) → squash (from the feet) → spin. The same picture at half resolution is the same character, smaller and chunkier.
- **Props**: the same pixel recipe (crosses, ticks, bells) or plain rounded shapes (bricks, presses, drawers, towers, tent cards, stamps). Data that rises out of a card is extruded from its printed shape, so it still reads from above.
- **Impact ring**: a thin accent-coloured ring spreads over the paper from an impact and fades in about a second.
- **Finish**: softness only behind the subject, a little glow on highlights only, a light vignette, slight warmth, a little contrast and saturation.

## 3. Colour logic

- **One light ground** (the mat) with a slightly darker dot, cool-tinted so the warm sun leaves it fresh; a warm wooden desk around it; cards in a near-white of the same temperature; **one dark ink** for print; **one accent reserved for what matters now**: the route, the current step's badge, the send button, the lit window, the key word.
- Two or three support hues for the cast and props, mid-saturated, never neon; one warm cast colour against the cool ground. **Red only for failure or trouble** (an error, a weak signal), **green only for success**; data in motion may take one bright support hue so it reads apart from the route.
- Judge the finished frame: avoid grey on grey; keep the vignette light and the shadows tinted cool.
- Two example palettes (starting points, not a menu):
  - *Lilac and violet* (creative tools, learning): paper `#e7e4f3`, dot `#cbc5e2`, card `#fcfbff`, ink `#1d1a2e`, mute `#807a96`, line `#e2dfee`, accent `#6b4ce6`; support apricot `#f39a5b`, sky `#5fb4e8`, lemon `#f2c94c`; red `#e2483d`, green `#3fa57a`.
  - *Cool grey and amber* (money, logistics, energy, food): paper `#e5e9ed`, dot `#c8cfd7`, card `#fdfdfc`, ink `#20252b`, mute `#7f8790`, line `#e0e4e8`, accent `#e8890c`; support teal-blue `#2f8fb0`, plum `#8a5a9e`, cream `#f4e3b5`; red `#d23a3a` (clearly apart from the amber), green `#3c9c6e`.

## 4. Type & on-screen text

- **In the world, only keywords and numbers**: step badges (an accent pill facing the camera, with a digit and one word), sizes, a name on a screen, a banner, a title card. Text meant to be read is framed clear of the narration bar, held still for its reading time, and large enough at the angle it is seen: flat print reads from 55–70° down; a low camera reads a standing tent card.
- **Titles are objects on the desk**: an opening tent card beside the first stop; at the end a large paper card dropped onto open desk in the final frame, where it covers no station, route or character. Leave that space when laying out the desk.
- **Three roles**: a mono for labels and numbers, a sans with a heavy weight for pills, titles and narrated lines, and the script face of the video's language that both fall back to (the DM Mono and Manrope kinds). The brand's own faces take these roles first. Pixels belong to the cast, so no pixel font on the print.
- **When the film explains something, a voice tells it**: short spoken lines, one idea each; the picture shows what the voice is saying at that moment, and the words on cards are only the keywords of the line.
- **Narrated lines sit in a quiet bar** low in the centre (a rounded ink box at about 72% opacity, bold, about 3.7% of the frame height in 16:9 and 4.5% of the width in 9:16), one line, never wrapped, each word arriving as it is spoken; the bar leaves with the line.
- **The camera keeps its subject about a tenth of the frame above centre**, so nothing that matters sits under the bar.

## 5. Motion quality

- **One steady pulse** of 16 frames (12 for a quicker film), so that a quarter is a whole number of frames; every action on its quarters (4 frames with a 16-frame pulse), big moments on the first pulse of a phrase of four.
- **Hops**: an arc with squash and stretch (stretch about 14% in the air, squash about 30% for 6 frames on landing, a small anticipation squash before take-off). Groups move follow-the-leader, each a quarter-pulse behind the one ahead, so they never close up.
- **Characters act**: they blink every couple of seconds, bounce on the pulse while they wait, squint when they are squashed, lean towards what they look at.
- **Pop**: sticker → toy in 8–15 frames (hinge up with overshoot, depth growing from the centre, a small jump, squash on landing). **Reverse pop**: toy → sticker or print when it is stored, delivered or taped down.
- **Slam**: drops from above easing in, lands on a pulse, squashes, jolts the card, sends out a ring.
- **UI comes alive in sequence**: digits, bars, ticks and typed words appear on quarter-pulses with an overshooting ease.
- Idle is a small bounce or a blink, never a dead hold of more than about 1.5 s. Paper and cards never move except for a jolt or a curtain-call hop.

## 6. Camera grammar

| Move | What it expresses | Can serve |
|---|---|---|
| Macro glide with a slow arc (close, 20–45° down) | being there, small scale | a character at work; one detail of an interface; a title standing on the desk |
| Whip from card to card (9–15 frames, banking into the turn) | the next step, cause → effect | a process moving on; a hand-off between tools |
| Rise to near top-down (55–70° down) | reading, one card as a whole | a screen, a counter, a table; any verdict |
| Ride along, low behind the subject | travel, momentum | a route; a delivery; a download |
| Pull back over the desk | the whole system, scale | a recap; the route so far; many stations at once |
| Hold with a shake | impact | a press, a stamp, a ring |

- One take, no cuts, no dissolves. The camera aims at live subjects and follows a smooth path between stops; drift is a breath, not a handheld wobble.
- Where a whip starts, the aim rests first (or the camera stutters). A character's take-off is the natural start of a whip. A whip between far-apart stops arcs up mid-way so the stops in between stay in frame.
- Wide shots frame the stations to fill at least 70% of the frame width. **The film closes on the whole desk**: every stop and the route in one frame, everything moving once, the title landing in open space.

## 7. Sound palette

- **Every action has a sound, in desk materials**: a pop is a short wooden knock with an upward sweep; a hop is a soft tick; a landing a felt thump; a press or stamp a low thump with a paper crumble; typing is dry key clicks; a delivery is one soft bell; a drawer is a wooden slide and a click.
- **Three weights on the pulse**: ticks for hops and UI, thumps for landings, one heavy slam for the press or the title card. Cues sit on the same quarter-pulses as the motion.
- **The camera's whip is a whoosh** sized by how far it travels, peaking as the camera arrives. Slow glides are silent.
- **A bed of a sunlit room**: quiet air and paper hush, never tonal, halved under the voice.
- **Pitch tells progress, not a tune**: tonal cues (the bell, a pop's sweep) share one scale, and a run of them rises a step at a time to the last stop.
- **Silence**: one quarter-pulse of nothing before the slam, and a breath before the title card drops.
- **Sound across the move**: the next station's sound (keys, a drawer) is heard a few frames before the camera reaches it.
- **Voice**: clear, friendly and unhurried, one idea per line; everything else halves while it speaks.

## 8. Native moves

- **Sticker pop.** A flat pixel sticker on a card stands up and thickens into a toy on a quarter-pulse. *Fits content like:* a new user, file or job appearing; a feature switched on; an order placed.
- **Reverse pop.** A toy lies back down into a sticker or a print. *Fits content like:* a photo delivered into a chat; a snapshot saved; a task archived.
- **Press.** A heavy plate drops on a toy; pixel debris bursts out and rolls away; the same character pops out at half resolution beside a counter that runs down. *Fits content like:* compression; a summary of a long report; a smaller bill.
- **Drawer.** A slab of a stacked tower slides open, a toy hops in, it closes and one window lights; small data cubes hop into the windows. *Fits content like:* storing something in the cloud; a queue; an archive.
- **Messenger.** A small pixel object (a bell, an envelope) flies an arc between two cards leaving a dotted trail, taps the target twice, and a banner drops on its screen. *Fits content like:* a notification; a hand-off; an alert.
- **Data run.** A stream of small bright cubes runs along the route between two stations. *Fits content like:* a download; a sync; a payment going through.
- **Blurry, then sharp.** A picture on a screen card is printed blurred, then snaps sharp as the toy lands in it. *Fits content like:* loading; focus; a draft becoming final.
- **UI rises.** Part of the printed interface grows solid out of its printed shape. *Fits content like:* progress; a metric climbing; a step badge.
- **Curtain call.** The closing pull-back frames every stop; each hops once in reading order, the characters pop back up, and a title card drops into open space. *Fits content like:* a recap; a team; a pipeline shown whole.

## 9. Pitfalls of the medium

- **Softness**: blur easily turns a subject or its words to mush. Blur only what lies behind the subject, lightly, never anything in front, and never anything meant to be read.
- **Print that goes soft when the camera closes in**: a card built small and scaled up is blurry. Lay the desk out at the size of its closest shot and pull the camera back for the wides.
- **Flat print read at a low angle**: text on a card seen from 20° is unreadable. Rise to 55–70° to read a card; put text the low camera must read on a standing object.
- **A standing toy hides the row behind it**: place characters beside or behind the text being read, or off the card, and check every reading frame for overlap.
- **Text under the narration bar or cut at the frame edge**: keep the subject above centre; show name plates and labels only in the shots that frame them whole; take camera-facing pills away before the camera moves on.
- **Screens that read as blank**: a phone card without a whole interface reads as an empty white slab in close-up.
- **Shadows of floating labels**: with the sun at 20°, a pill a little above a card throws a long shadow across its print. Labels and pills cast no shadow; no shadow falls across text being read.
- **Characters lost in the wide**: in the closing view every character still shows its eyes (at least about 2% of the frame width); otherwise frame closer, bring it forward, or scale it up for the curtain call.
- **Toys seen edge-on** read as stripes and lose their eyes: turn each towards the camera for its key moments.
- **Whips over bare paper**: arc a long move up so the stops in between pass through frame.
- **Dead stations**: a stop where only the main action happens looks static; give it one or two small moving things that do not compete with what the voice says.
- **Bars and ticks seen from above** turn into blobs: extrude them along the card or lay them flat.
- **Interpenetration**: followers move a quarter-pulse apart; a knocked-flat pile goes back to sticker thickness before it stacks; planes that cross each other flicker, so keep objects apart in depth.

## 10. Building it here

- **There is no 3D renderer here; the desk is CSS 3D.** One wrapper with `perspective` holds a `transform-style: preserve-3d` desk plane; the camera is that wrapper's `rotateX` (the angle down), `rotateZ` (the arc), `translate3d` and scale, driven by `springTo` (`SPRING.soft`) between stops and by `prog` with `ease.inOut` for whips. Keep the arc within about 40° either side.
- **Cards** are flat `div`s lifted with `translateZ`, with a few stacked edge layers for thickness (the way `Device` builds its body); the print is real DOM text, SVG and screenshots, never a scaled-down image.
- **The cast are stacked sprites**: the pixel picture (a CSS grid or an SVG of rects) repeated on 6–10 planes along its depth, darker towards the back, the inner layers larger than the outline, and one extra front plane for the eyes. The hinge is a `rotateX` at the feet; squash is `scaleY` from the feet. This reads as solid from the front three-quarters only.
- **Light is painted**: each object's shadow is a dark, blurred, skewed copy of its silhouette on the paper, stretched along the sun's direction and tied to the hinge and the jump height; lit and shaded edge layers get fixed warm and cool tints; window patches are blurred warm shapes with `mix-blend-mode: screen`; the haze is a gradient over the far desk.
- **Depth of field is blur by group** (`filter: blur()` on far props only, each drawn as one flat picture: a `filter` flattens the 3D inside the element it sits on); the impact ring is a flat `div` with a growing border radius. Leave out real shadow maps, glow passes and a free orbit behind the cast.
- **Motion**: hops are an arc from `prog` plus squash from `springAt(…, SPRING.bouncy)`; blinks and idle bounces come from the frame number and `noise(i, seed)`. The paper grain is one `feTurbulence` with a fixed `seed`; `Finish` adds the vignette at low grain.
- **Overrides**: one continuous scene, so no `SceneFrame` and `<SceneTrack sweeps={false} />`; no `Backdrop`. Leans on springs throughout.
- **The pulse lives in `timeline.ts`** (the unit, its quarter, and a helper that turns "phrase, pulse, quarter" into a frame); every hop, pop and camera key uses it, and `Soundtrack.tsx` places each cue on the same frame.

## 11. In a product video

- **The brand colour is the accent** (route, badges, the lit window, the key word); the brand's interface colours print on the cards; the ground stays light.
- **The real product is the print.** Each card's top is a real screenshot (a phone is a `Phone` laid flat on the desk with its screen, its own drop shadow switched off through `style`, since shadows here are painted), and a screen recording can play on a card at a top-down stop. Parts that rise out of a card are traced from the real screen, in its real colours.
- **The cast**: the brand's mascot redrawn as a small pixel sprite, or a neutral helper; kind, modestly drawn, never the butt of the joke.
- **Counters and sizes on cards are real figures**, each in `facts.md`; sample names and messages on screens are fictional and carry `<ExampleBadge />`.
- **End card**: the curtain call, then a paper title card with the logo and one line on how to get it drops into the open space.
- **Arabic**: lay the route right to left and run the curtain call that way; card heads carry one bold line with no spaced capitals; words appear whole on their quarter-pulse, never letter by letter; a rounded, heavy Arabic sans suits the pills and titles.
- **9:16**: lay the stations out as a column running away from the camera, read one card at a time, and close on a tall mat. Keep the narration bar above the bottom 22%, and badges and titles out of the top 12% and right 12%.

## 12. Variation space

You decide for each film: which apps or tools the cards are and how they lie on the desk, the cast, the route, the props at each stop, the palette within §3, the pulse within §5, the language of the voice.

- **Structures**: one item travels card to card and changes at each stop; the same job on two halves of the desk, slow against fast; one tiny detail that grows out to a desk of many cards.
- **Openings**: a terminal types a command and its cursor becomes a character; the camera falls onto the desk and lands on the first pulse; a title tent stands beside the first card while the hero pops up.
- **Endings**: the curtain call over the whole desk; the cast reverse-pops into a saved record; the camera rises until the cards become part of the dot pattern.
