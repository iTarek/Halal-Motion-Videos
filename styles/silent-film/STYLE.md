# 1920s Silent Film

> An engraved-illustration silent film projected in a picture house: ink line and silver wash, a 4:3 print that flickers and weaves between velvet curtains, intertitles instead of dialogue, and an effects table behind the screen that answers every action.
> References (grammar only): **Buster Keaton**, *The General* (1926) and *Sherlock Jr.* (1924) — the whole gag in one locked-off wide shot, a deadpan face, a body that does the acting; **Harold Lloyd**, *Safety Last!* (1923) — the near-miss; **Georges Méliès** — the iris and the substitution cut; **The Artist** (2011) — how few intertitles a modern audience needs; **1920s picture-house practice** — the film planned by mood section, one hand-made sound effect per event. Never use the Tramp look (bowler, toothbrush moustache, cane) or any real film's gags.

## 1. Essence, and what it is not

A one-reel film as it would look *projected*:
- **A 4:3 print in a gate between theatre curtains**; the rest of the frame is the picture house.
- **An ink drawing with washed greys and hatching**, printed on silver stock that flickers, weaves, scratches and burns at the edges.
- **Nobody on screen speaks.** The story is told by **action in wide shots** and by **intertitle cards**.
- **The sound is the room**: the projector in the booth, and a few hand-made effects that answer the action. A narrator, when there is one, reads the cards like a picture-house lecturer.

The premise here is the craft of telling a story without dialogue: deadpan comedy, a chase, a small act of kindness. Tender scenes are family, friendship and work well done.

Not a noir (no hard shadows as mood, no hard-boiled narration), not a sepia photo filter on modern animation (the drawing itself is engraved and inked), not a Chaplin pastiche.

## 2. Materials & rendering

- **Tonal painting, then ink.** Scenes are built in grey values only (print densities roughly: ink 0.07, dark cloth 0.15–0.3, mid wood 0.45–0.6, skin 0.8, white linen 0.9–0.95), with soft light gradients and a thin separation line. The **ink pass** then draws the whole frame: contours on the dark side of every edge, luminance posterised into about 5 soft wash steps, and 45° hatching (then crossed) in the darks, redrawn every 2–3 frames like hand-redrawn animation. Real footage and screenshots can go through the same pass so they share one hand with the drawing.
- **Print pass**: silver tone with a sepia amount between about 0.1 and 0.36, print black about 0.055 and white about 0.93, S-curve, halation on highlights, uneven density, flicker, gate weave with occasional perforation jumps, broken vertical scratches (white = scraped emulsion, dark = dirt on the negative), brown burn creeping from the edges, vignette, silver grain; then dust, a hair in the gate, a rare blotch.
- **Frame**: in 16:9 a 4:3 gate in the centre, a rounded aperture with a soft dark rim, velvet drapes at the sides lit by the screen's spill and by footlights. In 9:16 a 3:4 gate across the width, the drapes becoming a pelmet above and a stage apron with footlights below. Curtains can part and close.
- **Figures**: realistic proportions (7.5-head adults, about 5-head children) on a jointed rig that can turn a little toward and away from the lens; period clothes, modest and well covered; identity by silhouette marks (a hat, a garment, a tool).

## 3. Colour logic

- **Monochrome silver**, tinted only by the print: colder and harsher (more damage, less sepia) when things go wrong, warmer and cleaner when the film turns tender. Tint and damage are mood controls, set per section.
- Values carry everything: dark clothes against pale walls, pale faces against dark interiors; check every shot as a thumbnail.
- **One colour, at most**: one element may keep its hue through the print (still getting grain, flicker, vignette). Use it for the single thing the film is about, or not at all.
- Period tinting of whole scenes (amber for day interiors, blue for night, rose for a homecoming) is a valid alternative to sepia; one tint per section.

## 4. Type & on-screen text

- **The intertitles are the on-screen text.** A card of a few words holds about 2.5–3.75 s. With a voice-over, the card cuts in as its line begins and stays a moment after the last word.
- A black painted card with a deco border whose ornament follows the emotion: one heavy rule for a shout (the text shakes), a double rule with stepped corners and fans for narration, vines and small flowers for tenderness.
- Period faces: a small-caps display serif (the kind of Playfair Display SC) for titles and shouts, an italic old-style serif (the kind of Old Standard TT Italic) for narration and dialogue. The style needs these faces whatever the brand uses; the brand's typeface stays in the logo.
- A few words per card; three to five cards in a short film. A card never explains what the picture already shows.

## 5. Motion quality

- **Hand-cranked sampling**: character and camera motion hold each pose 2 frames (15 a second, close to the hand-cranked 16); flicker, weave, grain and scratches change on every frame. That mix makes it look projected rather than merely low-frame-rate.
- **Speed is an emotion**: undercrank chases and panic (the action runs a quarter faster or more, and the flicker with it); return to normal speed and a calmer print for tender scenes.
- **Deadpan performance**: the face barely changes; the body acts (crouch before effort, held poses, the two-frame snap of a double take, hands on knees). Objects act too (a wobble before a fall, a slow roll to rest).
- **Keyframed, eased poses**; only deliberate gags snap.

## 6. Camera grammar

A vocabulary, not a route. The period camera is heavy: most shots are locked. Opening and ending come from the topic.

| Move | What it expresses | Can serve |
|---|---|---|
| Locked-off extreme wide | cause and effect seen whole; the wait is the joke | a mechanical gag; a crowd; an elaborate process |
| Locked full shot | the premise in one frame | a character's routine; a face-off |
| Tracking shot with foreground passing | pursuit; momentum | a chase; a race; a delivery against the clock |
| Iris in / out | attention on one thing; a beginning or end of a thought | an object; a face; a memory |
| Half-open iris, held | a character sharing a look with the audience | a knowing glance; a surprise kept; complicity |
| Slow motivated push-in | tenderness; realisation | a child's first step; a letter read; a decision |
| Masked shot (keyhole, binoculars) | point of view; looking closely | a detective; curiosity; a birdwatcher |
| Split screen / double exposure | two places or two times at once | a phone call; a memory; a daydream |
| Insert shot (a letter, a ticket, a screen held to the lens) | the thing itself, readable | a message; a price; a proof |

Framing: wide and medium shots; big close-ups of rigged faces look like mannequins, so keep any close-up small or inside an iris. In 9:16 a gag that ran across the frame runs down it (stairs, ladders, a fall, a queue seen from above). Transitions: iris, fade to black, straight cuts, the curtain. No dissolves between shots of the same scene, no modern whip pans.

## 7. Sound palette

- **The projector is the ambience**: lamp click, motor spin-up, claw-and-motor purr, film flapping on the take-up reel. Quiet, steady, never tonal, and present from the first frame to the last.
- **The effects table**: one hand-made sound per event, dry and wooden, as a picture-house effects man would make it: a slapstick crack (a crash), a knock on a wood block (a door, a reveal), a ratchet (sneaking, a winding machine), sand blocks (a train, shuffling feet), coconut clops (hooves, hurry), a wind machine rising (a wait), a thunder sheet (a storm, a big machine), a bulb horn, a single desk bell (a glance at the audience), a dry snap (something breaking).
- **Three weights**: a tick or a ding for a look, a knock for a step of the chain, one crack or rumble for the gag's landing.
- **What stays silent**: walking, talking mouths, anything the picture already makes clear. Fewer effects than a modern film, each one exact.
- **Tonal accents**: a short falling glide for a fall, a low "boing" for a bounce; never more than a second.
- **Silence**: the effects stop and the projector alone remains; it frames a held moment. For the film break, even the projector stops.
- **Sound across a cut**: the motor spins up over black before the first image, and the wind machine of a wait runs on over the cut to what was waited for.
- **Voice**: optional. A picture-house lecturer reading the cards, warm and a little formal, few lines; the characters themselves never speak.

## 8. Native moves

A menu: use the ones your story needs.

- **The chain in one take.** A triggers B, B leaves the frame, the audience waits, B returns and hits C. *Fits content like:* a supply chain gone wrong; how a rumour spreads; a Rube Goldberg invention.
- **The near-miss.** A hand closes exactly where the object was a frame ago. *Fits content like:* catching a bus; a startup missing a deadline; a goalkeeper.
- **The intertitle twist.** A card reframes the next shot in a few words. *Fits content like:* "Meanwhile, in the lab…"; "The machine had other ideas."; a statistic as a punchline.
- **Undercranked chaos.** Speed and damage rise together. *Fits content like:* rush hour; a kitchen at dinner service; a warehouse on launch day.
- **Iris on the look.** An iris stops half-way so a character can glance at the audience. *Fits content like:* a product's knowing glance; a cat that knows; a scientist's surprise.
- **Redrawn footage.** Real video pushed through the ink pass becomes a period drawing; only footage the user owns or has the right to use, since redrawing it clears nothing. *Fits content like:* a company's archive; a family film; a club's own match footage.
- **The echo gesture.** A gesture from the start repeated by someone else with a new meaning. *Fits content like:* a teacher and a pupil; a handover; a tradition.

## 9. Pitfalls of the medium

- **Too much content kills a silent film**: a many-step chain at small figure scale is noise in a thumbnail. One legible chain with a pause in the middle beats three busy ones.
- **Puppet arms**: move the arm out from the body before bending it, let the shoulder rise and swing forward as the arm goes up, slim the torso's shoulder corner on the raised side, hide the arm's contour where it lies over the torso. Raise arms sideways (or turn the figure) so they don't cross the face.
- **Pose pops**: drive every figure by eased keyframe lists, never by `if` branches.
- **Floating props**: hold a prop between the palms at the depth between the arms; tie a rolling object's rotation to distance (angle = distance / radius).
- Brow angles flip with facing direction: check expressions in a crop at final size.
- An iris must track its target inside the gate after any camera zoom.
- A grab must put the hand exactly where the object is on its frame, or it doesn't read.
- Closed curtains lit only by a dark screen go pure black: add footlights.
- Heavy grain and damage hide small text → cards and inserts get a cleaner print than the action.

## 10. Building it here

- **Draw the ink directly in SVG**: paths with an ink stroke, five flat wash greys, and hatching as a 45° line `<pattern>` clipped to the dark shapes (a second, crossed pattern in the darkest). Shift the pattern and a light `feDisplacementMap` wobble from `Math.floor(frame / 2)`, so the drawing is redrawn while the poses hold.
- **Real footage and screenshots** take the ink pass as an SVG filter chain: greyscale, `feComponentTransfer` with a `discrete` table for the wash steps, an edge kernel in `feConvolveMatrix` multiplied on top, and the hatch pattern masked to the darks. There are no shaders here; keep the chain short and check it on a still.
- **Print pass**: one wrapper with `filter: grayscale(1) sepia(…) contrast(…)`, its amounts set per section; flicker, weave, scratches, dust and the rare blotch from `noise(x, seed)` on the frame number; a brown edge burn and your own vignette as gradients; `Finish` for the grain. A blurred, screened copy of the highlights gives halation.
- **Gate, iris, curtains**: the gate is a rounded `clip-path`; the iris an animated `clip-path: circle(…)` positioned in gate coordinates; the drapes are two SVG pieces that slide. Use `<SceneTrack sweeps={false} />`. Scenes take no `SceneFrame` entry or exit: its blurred push is the engine's default look, not this one. The timeline's `overlap` is how long the old scene stays mounted under the new one (8 frames by default): build it with `{ overlap: 0 }` for a hard cut, or give it the length of this style's own transition.
- **Figures** are jointed SVG groups posed from eased keyframe lists with `prog` and `ease.inOut`, sampled on the 2-frame hold. No springs, except `SPRING.snappy` for a deliberate gag snap. There is no 3D library: a figure's turn is a narrowing of its width and a shift of its limbs, not a rotation in space.
- **Cards** cut in and out on whole frames; nothing rises from a mask, so leave `MaskRise` and `Headline` out of this style.
- **Sections, cards and gags live in `timeline.ts`** (each section with its tint, damage and speed), and `Soundtrack.tsx` puts each effect on the frame of its event.

## 11. In a product video

- **This palette is the look**: silver, with the print's tint. The brand's colour is the film's one colour, kept for the product or the single thing the film is about.
- **The real product is an insert shot**: a screenshot or screen recording held to the lens like a letter, framed by the gate or an iris, under a light print (a little grain and flicker, no scratches across the text). In the wide shots the product is an inked prop in the character's hands; `Phone` supplies its screen.
- **Real numbers and claims go on intertitle cards**, a few words each, every one listed in the video's `facts.md`. Illustrative data carries `<ExampleBadge />`.
- **Keep the comedy kind**: deadpan, near-misses and machines with a will of their own; the character is never humiliated, and tender scenes are family and friendship.
- **End card**: a final intertitle with the deco border: the logo, one line on how to get it; then the iris closes or the curtains meet.
- **Arabic**: cards set in a bookish Naskh for narration and a bold Ruq'ah or ornamental Kufi for titles and shouts (weight and size replace small caps), with no letter-spacing; a shout shakes the whole line, never single letters. Chases run right to left.
- **9:16**: the 3:4 gate; one figure scale larger than in 16:9; cards and inserts in the middle of the gate, clear of the top 12%, the bottom 22% and the right 12%.

## 12. Variation space

You decide the genre (comedy, family drama, adventure, newsreel), the characters, the gag or the drama, the cards, the tint plan, the opening and the ending.

- Structures: **a newsreel** (a sequence of "reports" with headline cards and a narrator card between them); **a drama in three reels** (each reel a tint: amber, blue, rose; a setback card, a rescue); **a trick film** (one stage, an inventor, objects appearing and transforming by substitution cuts).
- Openings: **mid-chase**, the film already running and scratched, no title yet; **a single intertitle question** before any image; **the projectionist's view**: the beam from the booth, then the screen.
- Endings: **the film breaks** (the print burns through and the lamp shows white); **a final card that addresses the audience** directly; **fade to black on a locked wide** with the projector running on alone.
