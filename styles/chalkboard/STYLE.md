# Chalkboard

> A real classroom blackboard on which chalk writes itself, stroke by stroke in true writing order, with no hand, no chalk stick and no eraser in sight. One idea is taught the way a teacher does it: write what people think, wipe it with one pass, write what is true in the same place.
> References (grammar only): a primary-school reading lesson on a sliding double blackboard; the way a teacher underlines the parts of a word, circles one of them and wipes a wrong line. Take the order of a lesson, not any teacher's handwriting.

Suits short lessons on one idea: where a word comes from, a misconception set right, a worked problem. The length follows how many steps the lesson has.

## 1. Essence, and what it is not

- **A clean, freshly wiped board.** One even deep ink green with its own fine grain and very slow mottling. No history on the slate: no old swirls, no traces of old lessons, no floating dust, no bokeh.
- **Writing that grows like a hand writes.** Strokes appear along their centre line in the order a hand would make them, with pressure: pressed starts and corners, ends that lift to a point, feet that spread, hooks flicked off.
- **Three chalks at most.** White for structure, one accent chalk for the single part the lesson tracks, one pale chalk for a second hand (a pupil, a visitor).
- **A real object in a real room.** Wooden frame, a tray with sticks of chalk, painted writing lines or squares, sliding panels, a wall and a clock around it.
- **Few words, large letters.** The board holds keywords; the explanation is spoken.

Not Whiteboard Explainer: that style is a bright glossy board, coloured markers, floating marker props and magnets, faint marks of old lessons, and a camera travelling across a knowledge map of many regions to explain how something works. Chalkboard is a dark matte slate with nothing floating in front of it, kept clean, one question per board, the camera facing it flat; it advances by writing a claim, wiping it and rewriting in place, and it lives in a classroom (panels that slide, lines painted on, a tray, a wall). Not a slide either: nothing is typeset, nothing fades in.

## 2. Materials & rendering

- **Chalk on grain**: a stroke is revealed along its length with a round leading edge, and the board's grain decides which specks take the chalk, so every stroke is slightly broken, with a faint powder edge.
- Chalk edges are firm; loose powder only at the lift-off of a stroke (two to five grains). Never sugar the board.
- **Width and pressure**: each stroke carries a width profile (a bump at the start, bumps at corners, a taper on flicked strokes, a spread foot on pressed ones, a quick lift on hooks) and a pressure profile (heavier at both ends, lighter in the middle). Straight strokes bow slightly; horizontals rise 2–3° in the writing direction.
- **The room** (board, panels, frame, tray, clock, wall) is drawn once and never changes; the slate's grain belongs to the board and shows only when the camera is close.
- **Erasing** is one soft band along one smooth path, on a layer that holds only the strokes being wiped; it leaves a very thin, even haze. One wipe per film is plenty.
- **A wet streak** (a board just wiped with a damp cloth) is a soft shape with a wide blur at its drying edge, fading at top and bottom, that darkens the slate slightly; never a hard rim.
- **Light**: a gentle falloff away from the windows and at most a faint warm window patch. Light never draws shapes across the writing in close shots.

## 3. Colour logic

- Ground: deep ink green slate, wood frame, warm white wall and a coloured dado if the room is seen.
- White chalk carries every structure: words, lines, labels, arrows.
- The accent chalk (example: orange) is reserved for the one component the lesson tracks; it never decorates and never draws arrows or underlines that are not that component.
- A pale second chalk (example: yellow) marks another person's handwriting.
- No rainbow, no gradients in chalk, no glow. Painted lines are dull and thin so the chalk always wins.

## 4. Type & on-screen text

- **Board writing is handwriting, never typesetting.** The few big words and every symbol (arrows, plus, a tick) are hand-made centre-line strokes drawn by the chalk; labels can be a single-line handwriting face revealed along each letter in writing order. Check every hand-made word over a reference face before using it. This is the style's own hand and stays whatever the brand's fonts are.
- On the board: keywords only, one line, and at most a smaller second line under it.
- Sizes: big words 28–42% of the frame height in 16:9 (in 9:16, as wide as the board allows inside the safe area); label capitals at least 3% of the frame height; small writing gets heavier chalk, so no stroke is thinner than 3 px at 1080.
- A block is fully written before it is read, and stays fully in frame while it is read.

### Voice and narrated lines

- When the style tells a story it is narrated: one calm teacher's voice in short spoken sentences, said as the matching chalk appears; what the voice names is being written or shown at that moment.
- A quiet plate sits above the bottom safe area: the spoken line in a plain book face (a brand's text face fits here) on a soft dark plate, in and out with the voice. Long lines are split at sentence ends.
- Nothing the viewer must read on the board sits behind the plate; frames held for the board's words are composed above it.
- Pauses between voice lines are short (0.25–0.4 s); one longer hold (about 1.5 s) is kept for the verdict. Board action (a panel sliding, a wipe) runs under the voice instead of waiting for it.

## 5. Motion quality

- Continuous, on every frame; nothing is stepped.
- **Chalk speed**: a big letter takes about 3 frames per stroke including the air between strokes (a ten-stroke word about a second), labels about 1 frame per stroke. Writing speeds up rather than making the voice wait, but big writing faster than about 2 frames per stroke stops looking like a hand.
- When reading time and chalk speed pinch in a short film, change the words, not the hand. Ways that work (choose per film): fewer and shorter blocks on the board; big letters only for what the lesson tracks, everything else at label size; start a block while the voice is still on the previous line, so it is finished when it is named; write the next block while the last one is still being read in the same frame; hold the frame instead of moving on; make the film longer.
- Camera eases in and out (cubic), never bounces except a whip landing.
- Things appear only by being written, wiped, revealed by a sliding panel or by drying. Nothing fades in.

## 6. Camera grammar

The camera always faces the board flat on (no perspective tilt).

| Move | What it expresses | Can serve |
|---|---|---|
| Close shot on the writing | attention on how a word or line is built | a component; a step of a proof; a spelling |
| Slow pan along a line | time or sequence | a timeline; a derivation; a list of cases |
| Pull back to a fixed medium frame | the whole question at once | a comparison; a claim and its counter-claim |
| Same frame later | judgement | a correction; a before / after |
| Locked frame while a panel slides | a page turned in the room's own way | a new chapter; a hidden board |
| Whip with blur (half a second at most) | a jump to another part of the wall | a change of board |
| Cut on a line crossing a seam | a connection | cause and effect; the same part in two places |
| Wide shot of the whole wall | summary | the map of what was learnt |

The room is part of the style: at least once the frame opens wide enough to read board, frame, tray and wall as a classroom (where, and what else the room holds, is the film's choice). Close shots may show only slate and frame.

Rules: the subject is never smaller than a third of the frame in close shots; text blocks are never cut by the frame edge while they are read; no empty board for more than half a second. Transitions are hard cuts, a sliding panel or a line across a seam; no dissolves or page flips.

## 7. Sound palette

- **Voice first**: one calm teacher's voice in short sentences. It is the loudest thing in the film; everything else sits well under it and comes up only in the pauses.
- **Chalk**: a chain of short, dry, scratchy grains per stroke, as long as the stroke and as loud as the stroke is big, with a light knock where the chalk lands. The accent chalk is slightly softer and duller.
- **The room's moves**: a felt pass for the wipe; the low roll of a sliding panel ending on a stop. These are the two heavy sounds; a knock is the light one, a stroke the middle.
- **What stays silent**: the camera, the drying streak, the light.
- **Ambience bed**: room tone, very low. School sounds (a distant bell, a chair) only as punctuation.
- **Silence**: nothing but the room while a question or the verdict is read; the next stroke then sounds like an answer.
- **Sound across a cut**: the panel's roll starts a few frames before the cut to the locked frame, or the first strokes of the next board are heard over the end of the pull-back.
- **Tonal accents**: the one distant bell. Nothing else is pitched.

## 8. Native moves

- **Write, wipe, rewrite in place.** The wrong reading is written under its object, wiped with one pass, and the right one written in the same spot from the same camera position. *Fits content like:* a common misconception in physics; a misremembered date; a sum everybody gets wrong.
- **Tracked component in accent chalk.** One part keeps its colour and its height across several forms. *Fits content like:* a root through a word family; a variable through an equation's rearrangements; a gene through generations.
- **Sliding panel reveal.** The front panel slides away to show a clean back board, and can slide back to close the lesson. *Fits content like:* "but before that…"; the hidden proof; the answer kept back.
- **A line that sews two boards.** One accent line runs across the seam to join two places. *Fits content like:* the same constant in two formulas; a quote and its source; cause and effect.
- **Drying wet streak.** The freshly wiped board dries back and reveals what was left untouched. *Fits content like:* yesterday's question; a note left by a student; a reminder at the corner.
- **Second hand.** A different, childlike chalk colour and hand on the same board. *Fits content like:* a pupil's question; a visitor's comment; a wrong attempt.
- **Chalk as a measuring tool.** An accent object laid along a white line and ticked off, so a comparison is shown, not told. *Fits content like:* a unit's origin; a scale; how many of one thing fit another.

## 9. Pitfalls of the medium

- Dirty boards (old swirls, old traces, floating dust) read as "messy", not "real". Keep the slate clean and let the grain carry the realism.
- Equal-width lines look like a plotter and make letters misread. Use width and pressure profiles.
- Hand-made strokes that are slightly wrong read as another letter, and native readers catch it at once: check every word against a reference before using it.
- Small writing disappears with the same chalk width as large writing; give small text heavier chalk.
- Text written while the camera moves away is never read; hold the frame or keep the block in view.
- Accent colour spread over arrows and decorations stops meaning anything.
- A soft halo at the chalk's front reads as glow: a few grains at the tip, no gradient.
- A wet streak with a hard edge looks like a cut-out.
- Board-speak in the voice ("A plus B", part names with no meaning) is not understood: every component is said with what it means the first time it appears.

## 10. Building it here

- **Flat 2D: SVG for the chalk, CSS for the room, one wrapper `transform` for the camera.** No perspective, no 3D. Board, frame, tray, clock and wall are static `div`s and SVG; panels are `div`s that translate.
- **Strokes**: keep each hand-made stroke as a list of centre-line points with a width per point; draw it as a filled shape (or a few overlapping `stroke`s) revealed by arc length from `prog(frame, start, dur, (t) => t)`, with a round cap at the head. Labels in a handwriting face are revealed glyph by glyph with a short soft-edged `clip-path` wipe (in Arabic, one whole word per wipe: §11).
- **Chalk texture**: one SVG filter shared by all chalk, in board coordinates: `feTurbulence` with a fixed `seed` → `feComponentTransfer` as a threshold → `feComposite` to knock grains out of the stroke; a second, finer turbulence roughens the edge through `feDisplacementMap`. The slate uses the same grain at low contrast, plus a very slow drift of its mottling.
- **Wipe and wet streak**: the wiped layer sits under an SVG `<mask>` in which a wide blurred path grows along the wipe; a copy of the layer at a few percent opacity and blurred is the haze. The wet streak is a darker `div` with a blurred `mask-image`, shrinking as it dries.
- **Camera**: `prog` with `ease.inOut`; the whip gets a brief sideways `feGaussianBlur`. This style overrides the engine's springs, `MaskRise`, `SceneFrame` entries and the light sweep (`<SceneTrack sweeps={false} />`); no `Backdrop`, and `Finish` only for a light vignette. A hard cut needs the timeline built with `{ overlap: 0 }`: the engine's default leaves the old scene under the new one for 8 frames.
- **Stroke times are data**: every stroke's start and length live beside the copy and are scheduled from `timeline.ts`, so the voice's word timings can pull a block earlier and `Soundtrack.tsx` can place chalk on the same frames.
- **Sound in practice**: design three or four grain chains of different lengths and reuse them at varied levels; one cue per stroke for big writing, one per word for labels.

## 11. In a product video

- **The three chalks are the look; the brand's colour is the accent chalk**, in a chalky tint light enough to read on the slate. It marks the one thing the lesson tracks: the product's name, or the feature that answers the question. A brand too dark for the slate keeps to the logo.
- **Write, wipe, rewrite is the pitch**: what people assume (the problem), one wipe, then what is true, in the product's own words from its site or listing.
- **The product is taped to the board.** A real screenshot as a print held by four bits of tape, flat on, with a soft contact shadow, revealed by the sliding panel; a screen recording plays in the same rectangle (`<Device video={…} float={0} />` with no `moves`, so it stays flat). The chalk then teaches from it: a circle round a real feature, an arrow, a label.
- **Chalked numbers are real** and listed in `facts.md`. A worked example with made-up figures carries `<ExampleBadge />`.
- **End card**: the wide shot of the whole wall, lesson complete: the logo as a taped print, one chalked line on how to get the product, and a final underline.
- **Arabic**: the board reads right to left: writing, pans and the panel's slide all run leftward. Each word is chalked as **one whole joined word** in a single pass, then its dots are tapped on; never separate letters appearing one by one, and no letter-spacing. Use a classroom hand (a ruq'ah-style face). The tracked component is a colour change inside the intact word (a root's letters in accent chalk), never letters pulled apart.
- **9:16**: show one panel at a time and stack the lesson down the board (claim above, rewrite in the same spot, labels below); pans along a line become moves down the lines, and the room shot becomes a tilt from tray to clock. Keep the plate above the bottom 22% and writing out of the top 12% and the right 12%.

## 12. Variation space

You decide the topic, the board layout, the number of panels, the tracked component, the hands, the camera path and the pacing.

- **Structures:** a proof built on one board line by line; three wrong answers wiped one after another before the right one; two boards in dialogue, question on one, answer on the other.
- **Openings:** a last wipe crossing the board and leaving a formula; a close-up of the tray as one chalk starts writing; the bell ringing over an empty, freshly wiped board.
- **Endings:** the whole wall with the lesson complete; a final underline under one word; the board half-wiped for the next class with one line kept.
