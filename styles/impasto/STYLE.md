# Impasto Oil Painting

> Thick oil paint laid with a palette knife: big, flat, sharp-edged planes of colour, raked by light so every ridge of paint shows. Clarity first: value structure before texture, hard edges at the focus, calm planes everywhere else.
> References (grammar only): overhead shots of umbrellas crossing a wet street (a crowd's movement read as moving colour); palette-knife street paintings (Leonid Afremov and the genre around him) (knife mosaic, vertical wet reflections, warm lights against cool shadow); Sorolla / Sargent (few, decisive strokes and strong light); tightly sound-locked film editing (one action, one sound, one cut). Never copy their images, compositions or characters, and never name them in the film.

## 1. Essence, and what it is not

A moving oil painting made of knife strokes. Each stroke is a real object: a slanted plane of paint with a ridge where the blade pressed, a lip where it lifted off, broken streaks where the paint ran out, two colours dragged into each other. The world is **rebuilt from its strokes every frame**, but static paint never moves: only things that move in the story move. Light rakes across the relief, so the paint has body.

The look is clear and graphic (readable like a children's book at thumbnail size) but has the tactile surface of impasto when you look closely.

Not a Van Gogh swirl filter (strokes boiling everywhere), not a brush texture over flat vector art, not watercolour (paint is opaque), not a "painterly" photo filter (strokes are designed, not sampled from noise).

## 2. Materials & rendering

- **Value first.** Design each set as a flat, value-planned illustration (3 values minimum), then convert it to strokes. If the flat version doesn't read at a quarter of its size, no brushwork will save it.
- **Stroke sizes by importance and depth.** Coarse to fine: large planes in sky and walls, strokes of a few pixels only at edges and faces. Each shape sets its own largest stroke, level of detail and stroke direction.
- **Strokes never cross from one shape to another**, so silhouettes stay crisp. Lost edges are a choice: let two shapes share their strokes on purpose.
- **Thin underpainting** (the flat illustration itself, slightly blurred) under the strokes, so the canvas never peeks through as outlines.
- **Knife stroke**: longer than wide (about 1.7–3.1 × its radius long, 0.95–1.45 × wide), slanted ends, small angle and value jitter (about ±13°, ±6%). Solid objects that must read as silhouettes (figures, props) get little or no ragged run-out.
- **Light** rakes over the relief of the paint; wet or glossy surfaces get slightly more shine.
- **Reflections** (wet ground, glass, water) are **explicit stacks of short horizontal knife dabs** under each light source, broken and fading; never a painted rectangle.
- **No film grain**: grain plus compression eats the knife edges.

## 3. Colour logic

- Planes of mixed, slightly broken colour; each stroke may drag a second colour into the first. No gradients made by blending pixels: gradients are made by stroke-to-stroke steps.
- Warm light against cool shadow is the default temperature structure; the brightest, purest chroma is reserved for the story's subject (a prop, a crowd, a light source), not the background.
- **Colour can be an event.** Every stroke can hold a grey and a colour version; a monochrome world with one coloured exception, or a world re-laid from grey to colour, is native to the medium. The grey version is the luminance of the same strokes, slightly cool, with a small colour residue.
- Examples of palettes that fit: an after-rain sunset (cobalt → peach → gold sky, ochre facades, full-chroma props); a harvest field (straw yellow, burnt sienna earth, one viridian tractor); a night kitchen (umber shadows, sodium-lamp orange, one cold blue window). Choose from the topic.

## 4. Type & on-screen text

- Titles are **laid in with the knife** (a stroke-by-stroke sweep of about a second) and can be taken off by the world (washed by rain, scraped, painted over). They move with the world.
- Small text (credits, data, captions) stays crisp type over the painting: knife strokes can't carry small letters.
- A serif display face (the kind Cormorant is) for painted titles; the brand's own face for the crisp text.
- **Narrated lines**, if there is a voice: plain serif, at most two lines, set in a calm plane of the painting, each phrase appearing as it is spoken.

## 5. Motion quality

- Static paint is static. Only the things the story moves move (weather, figures, props, ribbons, light). Smooth motion on every frame, no stepping: clarity is the brief.
- Moving figures are strokes rebuilt each frame from **fixed seeds**, so their paint doesn't boil (walk cycles, reaching arms, props that open and close).
- **Actions sit on one pulse** (say 12 or 15 frames): a change of direction, a pop, a swell lands on it.
- Anticipation → action → follow-through on every gesture (lift before the strike, dip before the snap, swing after the stop).

## 6. Camera grammar

A vocabulary, not a route. Opening and ending come from the topic.

| Move | What it expresses | Can serve |
|---|---|---|
| Slow truck + push on a wide | a world observed; patience | a place; a routine; a crowd |
| Push-in through a silence | inner pressure | a doubt; a decision; giving up |
| Tilt-up from the ground or a reflection | discovery from below | a small character entering; a first sight |
| Pull-back from a close-up | reaction; the world answering | consequence; a reveal of who is watching |
| Rotating crane-out, overhead | pattern emerging from many | a crowd crossing; traffic; a gathering |
| Same wide, repeated | before/after | change of colour, season or time |
| Locked frame, light moves | time and weather as the actor | a day passing; a storm arriving |
| Macro slide along the paint surface | the matter itself | a texture; a crafted object; a map |
| Whip-pan into a new painted plate | energy, a jump | a list; montage; travel |

Framing: paint each plate larger than the frame and keep the camera **inside the painted plate**, or the bare canvas shows; in 9:16 the plate is painted tall (more sky above, more wet ground and reflection below), not cropped from the wide one. The subject gets the hard edges and the smallest strokes; everything else stays in big calm planes. Transitions: paint-over sweeps and scrapes, hard cuts on the pulse; no dissolves (two layers of knife ridges turn to mud).

## 7. Sound palette

- **Foley follows the paint and the world**: palette-knife scrapes on every repaint or paint-over, wet dabs, the materials of the scene (wet stone, nylon, wood, fabric, glass).
- **One action = one sound = one cut.** Three weights: a wet dab for a small stroke or a pop, a scrape for a gesture, a long loaded drag for a whole paint-over.
- **Beds in layers** (a hiss of rain or air, near grains, one distinct detail), quiet, never tonal, filtered when the camera goes under cover.
- **Pops can climb**: a sequence of events steps up in pitch, short accents and never a tune; leave the run one step short and let the film's last event complete it.
- **Still paint is silent.** Camera moves, raking light and held plates make no sound.
- **Silence**: one hard stop that cuts every bed to digital zero and holds at most one sound.
- **Sound across a cut**: the next scene's foley arrives inside that silence, before its picture.
- **Voice**, if any: warm and unhurried, someone describing a painting they love. Foley alone can carry a film without narration.

## 8. Native moves

A menu: use the ones your story needs.

- **Colour re-laid by the knife.** Every stroke keeps a grey and a colour version; a reveal wave re-paints them stroke by stroke. *Fits content like:* a town waking up; a patient recovering; a brand relaunch; a season turning.
- **The one coloured thing.** The world drawn in its grey version, one object in colour. *Fits content like:* one person in a crowd; the fruit that ripened; a warning light.
- **Motion paints the picture.** Moving objects drip their colour as knife dabs; seen from above, their paths leave a painted pattern. *Fits content like:* skaters on a rink; delivery routes across a city; a football play; migration.
- **Raking light.** At the peak, drop the light to a grazing angle so every ridge catches it, then let it settle. *Fits content like:* a sunrise; a moment of recognition; unveiling a sculpture.
- **Paint-over transition.** The next shot's strokes are laid over the previous one in a sweep, with a scrape on the soundtrack. *Fits content like:* a renovation; memory replacing the present; a timeline of versions.
- **Sound made visible.** A voice or a call becomes a paint ribbon that carries colour to what it touches. *Fits content like:* a message reaching its readers; a radio broadcast; good news spreading through a street.

## 9. Pitfalls of the medium

- Strokes confined to a shape shrink near its edges and leave canvas-coloured outlines → always the underpainting.
- Draw order matters: painting the ground after the buildings covers their feet.
- Plain rectangles read as posts or blocks → give silhouettes taper, a lit edge, and one or two defining dabs (shoulder, hair, handle).
- Proportions carry the object's identity at this stroke size: a prop drawn too wide reads as a different thing. Check silhouettes at thumbnail size.
- Film grain or heavy compression destroys the knife edges → no grain.
- Strokes that are re-seeded every frame boil → fixed seeds per stroke.
- A lighting filter over the whole frame lights the crisp text too → small type and screenshots sit above the painted layer, outside the filter.

## 10. Building it here

- **Strokes are data, drawn in 2D**: each one is `{ x, y, angle, length, width, colour, second colour, seed }`. Draw thousands on a `<canvas>` from the frame number, or hundreds as SVG polygons: a slanted four-sided shape, a linear gradient from the first colour to the second, a ragged far end cut with `clip-path` or a few extra points. No 3D renderer and no 3D library.
- **From a flat design to strokes**: build each plate as flat SVG shapes with a value plan, then fill each shape with strokes, coarse to fine, placed by `noise(i, seed)` (never `Math.random`) and clipped to the shape. Put the blurred flat design underneath as the underpainting.
- **Raking light is an SVG filter** on the painted layer: `feGaussianBlur` on the strokes' alpha makes a relief, `feDiffuseLighting` or `feSpecularLighting` with an `feDistantLight` lights it, and `feComposite` lays it back over the colour. Lowering the light's `elevation` over time is the raking-light move.
- **Events are per-stroke times**: a paint-over or a title "appears" stroke by stroke as a sweep passes each stroke's position; grey-to-colour is a mix from each stroke's grey to its colour as the wave reaches it (`lerp` per channel, or Remotion's `interpolateColors`).
- **Motion**: gestures take `springAt` with `SPRING.bouncy` (a clear swing past the stop) or `SPRING.snappy` (a slight one): the follow-through is the overshoot. `SPRING.soft` barely overshoots, so it suits heavy things that only settle; the camera is one transform on the plate with `prog(…, ease.inOut)`.
- **Overrides**: no grain (`<Finish grain={0} />` or no `Finish`), no `Backdrop`, no light sweep at cuts (`<SceneTrack sweeps={false} />`), no `MaskRise` on painted titles. Crisp text can use `Reveal`. A hard cut needs the timeline built with `{ overlap: 0 }`: the engine's default leaves the old scene under the new one for 8 frames.
- **Leave out**: true height simulation and per-pixel wet blending. The relief from alpha and the two-colour drag are enough at video size.
- **The pulse lives in `timeline.ts`**; `Soundtrack.tsx` puts each dab and scrape on the frame of its stroke.

## 11. In a product video

- **The brand's colour is the reserved pure chroma**: the one thing the painting's light falls on. The rest of the palette comes from the topic and stays broken and mixed, so the brand colour is the brightest thing in the frame.
- **The one coloured thing can be the product**: a grey world, and the product (or what it gives people) laid in colour.
- **The real product stays crisp**: a painted phone or laptop body in knife strokes whose screen is the real screenshot or screen recording, set in a calm plane like any small text. For a walkthrough, a paint-over sweep clears to a full `Device` or `Phone` and a second sweep returns to the painting.
- **Real numbers and claims only**, each in `facts.md`, set as crisp type on a calm plane; illustrative figures carry `<ExampleBadge />`.
- **End card**: the scene is scraped back to a calm plane of the brand's colour; the logo sits crisp on it (or is laid in by the knife if it is a simple mark), with one line on where to get it.
- **Arabic**: a painted title is laid in by a sweep running right to left, whole words at a time, never letter by letter and with no letter-spacing; choose a display Naskh or Thuluth-flavoured face with strokes thick enough to carry paint, and a plain Naskh for the crisp text. Trucks and paint-over sweeps run right to left.
- **9:16**: paint the plate tall, keep the subject and its hard edges in the middle band, and set titles and crisp text clear of the top 12%, the bottom 22% and the right 12%.

## 12. Variation space

You decide the structure, the cast (or none), the sets, the opening, the ending, the camera path, the pacing, whether there is a voice, and where colour lives.

- Structures: **one object, many hands** (a bowl or a boat passes through the people who make and use it, one painted plate per hand); **a still life comes alive** (a locked table whose objects move in turn, the light crossing it hour by hour); **a portrait in layers** (a face painted coarse to fine as a voice tells one life, each layer a decade).
- Openings: **the bare canvas** (underpainting only; the first knife stroke is the first event); **macro on a ridge of paint** that pulls back to become a mountain or a roof; **mid-action** (a figure already running across a finished plate).
- Endings: **scraped back** (the knife takes the painting off to the underpainting, leaving one mark); **the light goes out** (raking light sinks until only the ridges glow, then black); **the frame in a room** (the film was a painting hanging somewhere, seen by someone).
