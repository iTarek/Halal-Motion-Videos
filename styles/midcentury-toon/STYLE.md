# Mid-century Cartoon

> 1950s flat colour and limited animation for "follow along" films: colour printed a little off its broken ink line, bodies that hold still while hands do the work, abstract colour-block rooms, a calm classroom narrator, and the dry sounds of the things being handled.
> References (grammar only): UPA's early-1950s shorts (*Gerald McBoing-Boing*): colour and line on separate cels, geometric people, colour-plane backgrounds, limited animation. 1950s classroom films (Coronet, Encyclopaedia Britannica): direct address, one sentence per picture, numbered cards. Charley Harper and Mary Blair: soft atomic-age complements, geometric figures. 1950s appliance manuals: numbered medallions, curved arrows, round call-outs, sunbursts. Copy none of their characters, drawings, lettering or sounds, and never name them in a film.

A **scene style**: it performs a real use (a set-up guide, a how-to, a recipe card, a safety card) rather than telling a story. The viewer should come away able to do the thing. See section 12 for the jobs.

## 1. Essence, and what it is not

Flat, printed-looking colour with a thin, breathing ink line that never quite closes. **Every fill is a separate "cel" printed a little off its line**, re-rolled every 2–3 frames, so the picture shimmers like hand-registered paint while the camera glides. Light dry-brush tooth, paper grain, warm vignette.

People and things are geometric: egg or bean heads with the nose part of the face outline, simple, modest garments in two or three flat colours, four-finger cartoon gloves. **A presenter's body is a held drawing: only arms, hands, eyes and mouth animate.** Rooms are not drawn: a scene is one colour plane, a floor band, a horizon line with gaps, and one or two props.

Not the 1930s rubber-hose cartoon (no black-and-white, no bouncing world). Not a whiteboard explainer: it shows *how to do*, not *why*. The pictures are printed, not written on.

## 2. Materials & rendering

- **Off-register cels**: fill drawn with a small offset (about 0.4% of the frame's short side, down-right) from its line; the offset jitters by a pixel or two every 2–3 frames. Keep it on everything, text included; switch it off only for a crisp diagram.
- **Line**: ink ribbons of 0.3–0.45% of the frame's short side, with ±40% width breathing and 12–25% breaks. Characters in the middle of that range, props a little either side, walls in plans thicker and broken. Hands: one outline around the union of palm and fingers, thin finger separations.
- **Shading**: one flat darker plane clipped inside a shape, never a gradient. Drop shadows are a copy of the shape offset down-right in a paper shade at about 50%.
- **Ornaments**: 4-point atomic sparkles, 8–18-point starbursts, sunburst rays (about 30 wedges, semi-transparent), boomerang and kidney shapes, polka dots.
- **Grain**: light; print, not film.

## 3. Colour logic

- **A warm paper ground, a warm near-black ink, soft atomic-age complements.** Nothing pure or neon.
- **One ground colour per scene**: a lighter tint for walls, a darker shade for floors. Never place the product on its own colour, never the presenter on their own accent.
- **The product keeps one colour through the whole film**; the accent (a presenter's garment, stars, bullets) is a different hue. Step grounds rotate through the rest.
- Examples: *coral / mustard / turquoise* on cream; *tomato / olive / powder blue* on oatmeal; *plum / lime / sand* on off-white.

## 4. Type & on-screen text

- **Roles**: a 1950s script for a kicker (the kind of Oleo Script Bold); a slab serif (the kind of Alfa Slab One) for titles, numerals and headers, in ink over an offset colour plate; a geometric sans (the kind of Jost, medium weights) for body, details, labels and captions. A brand's own sans can take the body role; the script and the slab are the period, so they stay.
- **Caption card** for narrated lines: a paper-coloured rounded slab with a thin ink border (its colour cel offset too), small turning stars at the ends, sans at about 3.7% of the frame's short side, low and centred. Fades in quickly with a small rise as its line is spoken.
- **Step header**: an ink disc with a paper numeral, a small letter-spaced "STEP n OF N", and the step title with a cream plate; it slides in in steps.
- **Detail tags**: a paper card with a star bullet, popping on the action next to the call-out. Data lands on the picture, never as a slide.

## 5. Motion quality

- **Stepped drawing:** poses, hand shapes and the cel jitter hold 2–3 frames each (about 12 drawings a second). The camera, iris wipes, arrows, plan routes and rays move on every frame.
- **One pulse:** pick a unit in frames (say 15) and start every step, stamp and hand action on a multiple of it.
- **Held body, moving hands:** switch poses with a single in-between. A hand action has anticipation (pull back or lift), a fast action (5–8 frames, eased out), a follow-through (recoil, settle, lift away) and lands exactly on the pulse.
- **Pop-ins:** medallions, tags, labels and titles scale in with a back-ease (about 8 frames, strong overshoot).
- **Reaching the floor**: a kneeling pose whose arms are solved from shoulder to hand keeps the hands on the object while it moves; never stretch standing arms.
- **Lettering**: a script writes on with a clip wipe in its reading direction; headers slide in in steps.

## 6. Camera grammar

The camera is "the stand": never handheld. A vocabulary, not a route. Opening and ending come from the topic.

| Move | What it expresses | Can serve |
|---|---|---|
| Slow push (a few %) | Collecting the eye | a product on its stage; a result settling |
| Iris through a medallion | The number *is* the door | a change of step; a change of rule |
| Round call-out with a leader line | Context and detail at once | a hand action; a screen; a small part |
| Tilt with a pointing finger | The finger leads the frame | the one action that matters most |
| Push into a device, pan along signal rings | Person → interface → device | pairing; connecting |
| Iris from a round object into a top view | Circle to circle, function to plan | a button starting a job; a lid opening; a dial |
| Pull-back with counter-rotation from a detail to a plan | One lane becomes a pattern | a route, a schedule, a garden, a floor plan |
| Colour-block wipe (side, bottom-up) | Turning the manual's page | a tip; a summary; a new card |
| Split frame in two colour blocks | Right vs wrong, before vs after | a safety rule; an upgrade |
| Dead still ≥ 3 s | "This is the frame to screenshot" | a summary; a call to action; a QR code |
| Iris-out onto one object | The last circle | a closing on the product, the logo, the result |

Key subjects fill a third of the frame height or more and stay clear of the caption band. Transitions are circles, colour-block wipes and cuts on the pulse; no dissolves.

## 7. Sound palette

- **Foley by material**: cardboard creaks and thumps, appliance plastic knocks and clicks, floor scrapes, glass and ceramic clinks, pours, damped rubber stamps for medallions, paper swishes for wipes, pen scratches for a route, an iris "shhk".
- **One hand action per step, one sound per hand action**: the click, the pour or the snap *is* the step. Held bodies, the gliding camera and the cel shimmer stay silent.
- **Tonal accents, never a tune**: each medallion stamps with one soft bell note, a step higher each time, and the summary brings the notes back one per item; a short rising glide for a dimension arrow; rising pips for "connected".
- **Ambience**: warm room tone (low, soft noise, very quiet) and a mantel-clock tick on the pulse; a device's motor hum can take over when it works.
- **Silence as a tool**: cut *everything*, ambience included, for the pulse before the most important action, so the first sound after it is the step. Room tone alone before a summary.
- **Sound across a cut**: a narrated line starts a few frames before its iris opens, and the tail of a stamp or a clink rings on over the next card.
- **Voice**: calm classroom authority, one clause per line ("Step one. Set it against a wall."). Check each key noun in what `npm run review` prints under "Heard:"; an accent can blur it.

## 8. Native moves

Each move serves information. A menu: use the ones your film needs.

- **Off-register colour** makes an info card hand-made and warm. *Fits content like:* museum rules; a clinic's sign-in screen; hotel check-in.
- **Limited animation points the eye**: the still body leaves the hand as the only mover, and the hand *is* the step (one hand action per step, on the pulse). *Fits content like:* threading a sewing machine; changing a bike tyre; setting a thermostat.
- **The round call-out** is the close-up; the step's data lands inside or on it on the action. *Fits content like:* a dosage on a measuring cup; a torque value on a bolt; a code on a keypad.
- **The numbered medallion** marks hierarchy and opens the next step; it stamps onto the outgoing subject and the camera goes through it. *Fits content like:* a pool's five rules; a checklist for a flight; stages of a recycling sort.
- **The payoff turns function into ornament**: a real process (a route, a watering schedule, a seating plan) draws itself region by region and, seen whole, reads as an atomic-age textile. *Fits content like:* a sprinkler's coverage; a delivery round; a week of medication.

## 9. Pitfalls of the medium

- A route that ignores obstacles → follow the product's real rules; flow streamlines bend around obstacles and never cross. Lay it one region per pulse, not all at once.
- A separate nose wedge looks glued on → one open curve from the bridge round the skull, closed at the nose tip.
- Thin arms read as sticks from the neck → shoulders at the torso corners, sleeves as a thick ink ribbon with a colour ribbon on top.
- Fat-blob fingers turn the hand into a cloud → stroke every part first, fill second (one union outline), slender fingers with separation lines.
- Zooming the world triples the ink → close-ups in call-out circles drawn at their own scale.
- Detail text without reading time → move the data into the detail, the context into the title and the voice.
- A scene seen through an iris before its own start shows a negative local frame → clamp its local frame to zero.

## 10. Building it here

- **Everything is SVG inside React.** One small `Shape` component of your own draws any shape twice: the fill path, translated by the register offset, and the ink path on top with `stroke-linecap: round`. Text gets the same two plates.
- **The breathing, broken line**: an SVG filter (`feTurbulence` with a fixed `seed` into `feDisplacementMap`) on the ink layer for the width breathing, and a `stroke-dasharray` built from `noise(i, seed)` for the breaks.
- **Stepped drawing**: `const step = Math.floor(frame / 2)`; poses, the register jitter (`noise(step, seed)`) and the filter seed read `step`, while the camera wrapper, irises and routes read the real frame.
- **Irises and wipes**: `clip-path: circle(…)` on the incoming scene for an iris, `clip-path: inset(…)` on a colour plane for a block wipe. Use `<SceneTrack sweeps={false} />`: the light sweep is not part of this look. Scenes take no `SceneFrame` entry or exit: its blurred push is the engine's default look, not this one. The timeline's `overlap` is how long the old scene stays mounted under the new one (8 frames by default): build it with `{ overlap: 0 }` for a hard cut, or give it the length of this style's own transition.
- **Call-outs**: a round `overflow: hidden` container that holds its content at its own scale, an ink ring and an SVG leader line.
- **Motion**: pop-ins with `prog(frame, start, 8, ease.back)`, hand actions with `ease.out`; the arm from shoulder to hand is two lengths and a little trigonometry. No springs on bodies: they are held drawings.
- **Leans on** `Finish` for the light paper grain; its vignette is neutral black, so keep it faint and add the warm one yourself as a `radial-gradient`. `MaskRise` and `Headline` are not this style: script wipes on, slab headers slide in.
- **The pulse and each step's frames live in `timeline.ts`**; `Soundtrack.tsx` puts the stamp, the hand's sound and the bell note on the same frames.

## 11. In a product video

- **The brand's colour is the product's colour**, the one hue the product keeps through the film. The accent and the step grounds are other hues from the soft atomic-age range. A neon brand colour is toned for the drawn product and used pure only in the logo.
- **A physical product is redrawn** as a geometric shape in that colour, with its real buttons, ports and parts in the right places. **An app lives in the round call-out**: the real screenshot in a flat `Phone`, ringed in ink, while the drawn hand points at the control; the screenshot changes on the frame the finger lands.
- **Every step detail is real**: quantities, times, settings and the order of steps come from the product's own guide and are listed in `facts.md`. A sample name or number on a screen carries `<ExampleBadge />`.
- **End card**: dead still for 3 s or more: the logo in a starburst on a clean ground, one line on how to get it, and a QR code only if the user supplies the real link.
- **Arabic**: mirror the layout (presenter, arrows, leader lines, step header) and wipe the script on from the right. Words appear whole, the "STEP n OF N" line loses its letter-spacing. Choose a flowing display script for the kicker (the kind of Aref Ruqaa), a heavy display face for titles and a geometric Arabic sans for the body.
- **9:16**: one tall colour plane with the presenter or product in the middle band; the call-out goes above or below instead of beside; a split frame stacks top and bottom. The step header sits under the top 12%, the caption card above the bottom 22%, nothing important in the right 12%.

## 12. Variation space

You decide the thing, the presenter (or none), which steps and in what order, the opening, the ending, the camera path, the colours and the pacing. Use cases are **grammar for the order and duration of information**; build your own timeline from the user's facts.

| Use case | Information order | Hold per layer | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Product set-up guide** | product name → title → step title → step detail on the action → payoff → tip → call to action | title ≥ 4 s; step title the whole step; detail long enough to read twice; summary ≥ 3 s clean | 30–40 s, 2–5 steps |
| **Safety / rules card** | rule number → rule → one "why"; a held figure, a medallion, a gesture, a pictogram per rule | 3–4 s per rule | 20–40 s, 3–6 rules |
| **Recipe or routine card** | step → quantity/time → result; side view, call-out on the hands, result as a top-view pattern | 3–5 s per step | 30–45 s |
| **Onboarding for an app or service** | screen action → setting value → confirmation stamp; the screen lives in the call-out | 3–4 s per action | 20–30 s |
| **Store or event notice** | headline → time/place → call to action; colour-block wipes between cards | ≥ 3 s per card | 15–25 s |

- Structures: **a rules card with no product**, one held figure and a medallion per rule; **a recipe on a tabletop**, seen from the side, ending in a top view; **a do / don't split**, each rule shown twice in two colour blocks.
- Openings: **the finished result first**, then "here's how"; **the problem in one gesture** (a hand fumbling a cable); **a numbered list** stamped on, then opened item by item.
- Endings: **a checklist ticking itself**; **the hands at rest** beside the finished thing; **a QR code** held dead still in a starburst.
